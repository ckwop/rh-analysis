Key ideas of the claimed proof

A narrated tour with live experiments. Press Play narration below; each paragraph is read aloud and highlighted. The proof itself is still being checked: nothing here says it is correct.

0The question

The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about the zeros of the zeta function, but there is an equivalent statement about primes, and that is the one this tour uses. Count the primes up to y, each weighted by its logarithm. The answer is roughly y. The Riemann Hypothesis says the error is never much bigger than y.

The generalised version says the same for primes in arithmetic progressions, which is measured by Dirichlet characters. So everything in this tour is about one thing: showing that certain sums over primes have only square-root sized errors.

1Square-root cancellation

Here is the error for real primes. The blue curve is the prime count minus y. The orange curve is the same sum twisted by the character modulo 4, which counts primes p≡1 (mod 4) as plus and p≡3 (mod 4) as minus. The grey lines are ±y.

Both errors stay inside the square-root band. That is what the Riemann Hypothesis predicts. A computer can show it up to any height we like, but no finite computation proves it. A proof has to explain why the cancellation happens.

2The model: curves over finite fields

There is one setting where the analogue of the Riemann Hypothesis is a theorem: curves over finite fields. Take the elliptic curve y2=x3−x, and count its points modulo a prime p. The count is p+1−ap, with an error ap. Hasse proved that |ap|≤2p.

The plot shows ap for every prime p≤3000, between the curves ±2p. Look at the primes p≡3 (mod 4), drawn in orange: their error is exactly 0. These are the primes that are inert in the Gaussian integers, where the curve is supersingular. Remember them. Inert primes come back as the source of the whole method.

p≡1 (mod 4): splitp≡3 (mod 4): inert, always 0

3Stepanov's idea: vanish to high order

In 1969 Stepanov found an elementary proof of the finite field case. Build an auxiliary polynomial that vanishes at every point of the curve, not just once, but to order M. A polynomial cannot have more zeros, counted with multiplicity, than its degree. So M×(number of points)≤degree.

Making the polynomial vanish to higher order costs degree. The bound comes out as roughly qM+M: the first term shrinks as M grows, the second term grows.

On the chart, the horizontal axis is the vanishing order M and the vertical axis is the size of the bound, both on a log scale. At the left, with M small, the curve sits on the grey line: no better than the trivial bound q. At the right, the cost of vanishing takes over and the curve climbs again. In between it dips to the green line, at M=q, where the bound is about 2q. That dip is the Riemann Hypothesis for the curve. Move the slider to try other sizes of q: the dip is always at the square root.

4From curves to the integers: Frobenius classes

Over the integers there are no points to count. The claimed proof replaces points over larger and larger fields with primes sorted into classes, called Frobenius classes, of a large abelian number field. The simplest example is sorting odd primes by their residue modulo 16.

The bars show, for each class, how far its prime count is from the average. The deviations are small, about the square root of the total. The proof needs only upper bounds for these classes, and only for the inert ones, drawn in orange. A positivity argument on the finite group of classes turns upper bounds into two-sided bounds, the same way it does for curves.

5Where multiplicity comes from: divisibility

Stepanov needed vanishing to high order. Over the integers the analogue is divisibility by many primes. The claimed proof gets it from elliptic functions with complex multiplication. Take the lemniscatic sine and its n-th derivative at 0. At an inert prime p, that number is divisible by a high power of p, growing in proportion to n.

On the chart, the horizontal axis is n, which derivative we take. The vertical axis is how many times the prime divides the resulting number. The orange line is the exact power of 3, computed for every n≤2000. It climbs in a straight line, about 38n. So the more derivatives we take, the more divisible the number becomes, in proportion. That proportional growth is the integer version of vanishing to high order.

The thick grey line under it is the prediction ⌊n3⌋+⌊n27⌋+⌊n243⌋+⋯, using only the odd powers of 3. The orange line sits on it. The dashed line above is the power of 3 in n!, which uses all the powers. So about 34 of the factorial's divisibility survives. The blue line, for the split prime 5, stays at 0. That is why inert primes matter: their formal group has height 2, and that is what makes the divisibility grow.

6Counting divisibility against size

Stepanov compared zeros with degree. The integer version compares divisibility with size, using lattices and Bost's slope inequality: a non-zero integer divisible by many primes must be large. The proof builds a chain of m theta lifts. Each lift adds divisibility, so the gain grows like m, while the cost of the chain also grows like m.

The result is the same trade-off as in Stepanov's case, now with the size x of the primes in place of q, and the chain length m in place of the vanishing order. On the chart, the horizontal axis is m and the vertical axis is the size of the error, both on a log scale. With a chain of length 1, the curve starts at the grey line: the trivial bound, about x. As the chain gets longer the error falls, until the cost of the chain takes over. The lowest point is at m≈x, and there the error is close to the green line, x, up to a small power of x. That is exactly the quality the Riemann Hypothesis needs. Move the slider: however large x is, the best chain length is about x.

7The threshold, and why it does not matter

The estimates of section 6 do not hold for every x. They come with a condition: x≥(2o)8/ε. Here o is the order of the character, and ε measures how close to 12 we want to get. For the zeta function o=1, and ε<152, so the smallest the condition can ever be is 28×52=2416.

Nothing about the primes changes at this point. The condition comes from one step of bookkeeping. Only a fraction 14o of the Frobenius classes are inert classes that the method can use, so the proof loses a constant factor. To keep the final bound in the clean form x1/2+small, it hides that constant inside a tiny power of x. It needs xε/8≥2o. A tiny power of x only beats a fixed constant once x is enormous.

The first chart shows this. The horizontal axis is the size of x, measured by its number of binary digits. The rising blue line is xε/8; the flat orange line is the constant 2o. The bound is certified only to the right of the crossing, in the shaded region. Move the sliders. A smaller ε, which means a sharper result, pushes the crossing further out, and as ε→0 the threshold grows without limit. So there is no single cutoff. Each ε has its own.

What happens below the crossing? Nothing different. The primes there behave just as well, as the numerical checks in section 1 show. This chain of inequalities simply does not cover them. It is like proving that n2≥1000n. The easy argument works from n=1000 onwards and says nothing about smaller n, although nothing special happens at 1000. The proof also has other thresholds, where good pairs of primes and chain data are shown to exist. Those are proved to exist but never computed, so 2416 is only the explicit part of the true starting point.

Why is a bound that only starts at a huge x good enough? Because a zero off the critical line cannot hide. If there were a zero with real part β>12, the prime-count error would swing up to about yβ, again and again, for arbitrarily large y. The second chart shows this. Both axes are logarithmic, so powers of y become straight lines. The orange line is the swing caused by such a zero. The green line is the proved bound y1/2+ε. As long as β>12+ε, the orange line is steeper, so it eventually crosses the green line and the bound would be broken. Move the slider: the closer β is to 12, the further out the crossing, but it always comes. If β is inside the margin, take a smaller ε.

swing caused by a zero with real part βproved bound y1/2+ε with ε=0.02

There is also a precise reason why small values cannot matter. The final step reads off the zeros from the poles of an integral, the Mellin transform ∫1∞B(y)y−s−1dy of the error B. Changing the error on any finite range [1,X] changes that integral only by ∫1X⋯, a function of s with no poles at all. So a finite range can neither create a zero nor hide one. That is why the proof can ignore small x, and also why no finite computation could ever prove the hypothesis.

Putting this together: for every ε>0, the proof shows there are no zeros with real part above 12+cε for a fixed constant c. Each ε has its own threshold, and none of them needs to be checked by computer. Letting ε→0 leaves no room for any zero off the line, which is exactly the Riemann Hypothesis.

8Putting it together, and what is still open

Assemble the pieces. Upper bounds for inert Frobenius classes, plus bounds for a few auxiliary twisted characters supplied by well chosen pairs of primes, feed a positivity criterion. That criterion forces every Dirichlet L-function, and the zeta function, to have no zeros to the right of the line Re(s)=12.

As section 7 showed, what has to be checked is not numbers but reasoning: that each definition means what it claims to mean, and that every step from the starting facts to the final statement really follows. The authors wrote the whole proof in Lean, a program for checking mathematics. In Lean every definition and every step of every proof is written in a precise formal language, all the way down to the rules of logic. Lean's checker, a small core program, replays each step and accepts it only if it follows exactly from what came before. Nothing is taken on trust except 3 standard axioms that the rest of mathematics also uses.

Checking the claim therefore means three things. First, compile all 175,187 files: compiling is what makes Lean check every proof in them. Second, ask Lean which axioms the final theorem depends on. The answer must be only the 3 standard ones, with no unproved assumptions slipped in. Third, confirm that the final theorem states the real Riemann Hypothesis, using the standard definition from Mathlib, Lean's shared library of mathematics, and not a look-alike. The definition passes the third test so far. Several key stages already pass the second. The compilation is about half done, with no errors in the mathematics. Only when all three tests pass for the final theorem would the claim deserve to be taken seriously.
